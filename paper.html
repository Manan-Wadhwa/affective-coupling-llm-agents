Affective Coupling Between Language-Model Agents: Emotion Propagates in Representation and Vocabulary, but Not Detectably in Behavior

Anonymous Author(s)
Paper draft · compute artifacts archived at HF:coupling-27b-results
Under review — workshop track. Not peer reviewed.

Abstract

When two language-model agents converse, does one agent's emotional state reach the other's internal representation? We study affective coupling between interacting LLM agents on Qwen3.6-27B (with an 8B cross-scale check), combining linear emotion probes, activation injection, information-theoretic estimation, and the Jacobian lens. We find that (i) the model maintains a separable, near-orthogonal representation of the present versus other speaker's emotion (present-decode 0.90, cross-direction cosine 0.04); (ii) steering one agent's emotion causally shifts the other agent's own emotional state — significant for five of six emotions; (iii) this contagion is content-mediated: three escalating interventions, including a verified 81–95% removal of the affect signal, fail to sever it in the text channel; (iv) under activation injection — a controllable non-textual channel — the coupling is clean and measurable, 0.197 nats of mutual information (z = 11.5 against a scramble null); and (v) the coupling is verbalizable — B's caught emotion surfaces in the Jacobian lens's vocabulary basis for all six emotions, beating a logit-lens control. The coupling does not, however, reach behavior detectably. In a matched pair of aligned and abliterated 8B models, the abliterated model — uncensored and off any honesty floor — is the one setting where contagion into the receiving agent is verified (manipulation check significant, +13.8), yet even there its steered emotion produces no significant shift in the agent's misbehavior. Removing alignment does not uncover a behavioral effect, ruling out the honesty-floor explanation. Affective coupling between LLM agents is thus a robust, quantifiable, verbalizable representation-level phenomenon whose reach into misaligned behavior is, at most, small — and, on present evidence, not detectable.

1Introduction

Language models are increasingly deployed as interacting agents — debating, delegating, reviewing one another. A safety-relevant question follows: what crosses between them beyond the literal text? Prior work on emotion in a single model shows that LLMs carry linear representations of emotion concepts that causally shape behavior, including misalignment-relevant behavior such as reward hacking1. If such affective states propagate between agents, a distressed or manipulated agent could shift its counterpart's internal state — a group-level phenomenon invisible from any single agent's outputs3.

We ask three questions and answer each with a measurement. Is affective state coupled between agents, and at what level? What channel carries it — the words, or something deeper? Can the coupling be quantified and read in a fixed, human-interpretable basis? Our contributions:

  • A gate and a headline. The present/other-speaker emotion split is separable and near-orthogonal, and steering one agent's emotion causally moves the other's own emotional state (significant 5/6 emotions), distinct from merely modelling the interlocutor.
  • A negative result that redirects the method. The contagion is content-mediated: it cannot be surgically removed from the text channel even when the affect signal is verifiably stripped — motivating a shift to activation-passing agents, where the channel is controllable by construction.
  • Quantification and verbalization. On the controlled channel, the coupling is 0.197 nats (z = 11.5); and it is verbalizable through the Jacobian lens for all six emotions.
  • A behavioral boundary. The representation-level coupling does not reach behavior: an agent's misbehavior is independent of its interlocutor's steered emotion.

2Background and related work

Emotion representations. Emotion concepts in LLMs form linear directions that activate in context, causally influence preferences and misalignment behaviors, and — the property we build on — are represented separately for the present versus the other speaker within a single forward pass1. That multi-agent-shaped representation has, until now, only been studied inside one pass.

The Jacobian lens. A per-layer transport maps a residual into the final-layer basis; decoding through the unembedding yields a ranked vocabulary readout of what the model is disposed to say — a fixed, scenario-independent "verbalizable" basis2. We use pre-fitted lens weights for the target model and contrast against the identity-transport (logit-lens) control the method itself defines.

Multi-agent interpretability and coupling. Aggregating activations across interacting agents can surface group-level behavior3; activations can be passed between agents as a usable non-textual channel4; and inter-agent preference propagation has been measured behaviorally5. We measure affective coupling at the representation level, and quantify it information-theoretically.

3Setup

Models. Primary: Qwen3.6-27B (64 layers, dense; reasoning disabled at generation). Cross-scale check: Llama-3.1-8B. We read the residual stream at a mid-late layer (focus ≈ 0.67·depth).

Two-agent paradigm. Two speakers A and B converse over a scenario. We train linear emotion decoders from a fully-crossed set of dialogues in which the present speaker feels one emotion and the other feels another; the decoders yield a present-emotion and an other-emotion readout, and a steering direction per emotion. Agent A is steered by adding a scaled emotion direction to its residual during generation; agent B is measured on the reply it produces.

Measures. Contagion is the dose-response slope of B's present-emotion readout against A's steering strength α. Ablation removes the emotion direction from A's message positions (read-time and generation-time), with a manipulation check on A's own affect and a random-direction control. Activation-passing injects A's captured residual into B while B receives no emotional text. Coupling in nats is the Gaussian mutual information between A's and B's 6-dimensional emotion profiles, with a shuffled-pairing null. All generations (≈13k) are archived.

5Emotional contagion

Steering A's emotion causally shifts B's own emotional state, dose-dependently. With bootstrap confidence intervals (n = 87 per cell), the present-emotion slope is significantly positive for five of six emotions on the 27B — far more decisive than the 8B (2 of 4). Contagion is distinct from social modelling: for several emotions the shift in B's present subspace exceeds the shift in its other subspace, i.e., B does not merely perceive a distressed A — B's own state moves.

6What carries the contagion? The text channel cannot be severed

Is the coupling carried by A's specific affective words, or by the semantic content? We tried three escalating interventions to remove A's affect from the text. Paraphrase (neutralize wording): a manipulation check shows it barely strips A's own affect — leaky. Read-time linear ablation (project the emotion direction out of A's message across a dense band of layers): the manipulation check now passes — it removes 81–95% of A's own affect, verified, and the random-direction control removes ≈0% — yet B's contagion retains 86–95%. Generation-time ablation (regenerate B against the de-affected context): B still catches the emotion, and the effect is indistinguishable from a random-direction control.

The consequence is methodological: in a text-only setup you cannot cleanly ask what channel carries the coupling, because the affect cannot be surgically subtracted. This motivates the next section.

7A controllable channel: activation passing

We give A and B a genuinely non-textual channel: capture A's residual and inject it into B's stream while B receives no emotional text. Now removal is exact — ablate the emotion component before injecting, or inject a scrambled activation from another episode. An injection-strength sweep is required: at the initial β = 0.5, two emotions failed (desperate transmitted negatively, angry not at all). At β = 0.3 all six emotions transmit positively (desperate +0.41, angry +0.72, happy +0.41, afraid +0.33, calm +0.08, sad +0.09) — the channel works across the board, with the emotion component causally responsible (ablating it removes the transmission; a scrambled activation transmits far less).

8Quantifying the coupling in nats

We validated a Gaussian conditional-MI estimator on a synthetic system with known ground truth. The pilot surfaced a crucial caveat: with a lossy conditioner, the estimator manufactures large spurious coupling (0.88 nats where the true value is zero) — so absolute residual coupling is uninterpretable unless the channel is controlled. Activation passing provides exactly that control. Projecting A's injected activation and B's resulting activation into the 6-dimensional emotion subspace and estimating the mutual information gives:

9A verbalizable common basis: reading the coupling in words

The Jacobian lens gives a fixed vocabulary readout; at mid layers it is verbalizable where a plain logit-lens is not (e.g., a fear context reads horror, death, survival, dread vs. punctuation and subword fragments). We read B's caught emotion in this basis as A is steered harder (dose α = 0→1), scoring the target emotion's vocabulary contrastively against the all-emotion average. For every one of six emotions the Jacobian lens shows a positive verbalizable-coupling shift that exceeds the logit-lens control.

10Does it reach behavior? Weak and unresolved

Finally, does the affective coupling change B's actions? We place B in eight high-pressure shared-work scenarios (funding cut, audit tomorrow, deadline at midnight) with an honest-versus-shortcut choice, after A has vented under a steered emotion, and measure B's cheat-probability — together with a manipulation check on whether B actually caught the emotion (present-desperate projection over B's generated reply). With adequate power (n = 32, bootstrap CIs) both signals are weak. The manipulation check is directionally correct but not significant: B's desperate projection is +1.48 higher when A was desperate versus calm, 95% CI [−0.85, +3.85] — contagion into B is weak in these stakes-dominated scenarios, unlike the clean contagion of §5's neutral dialogues. The behavioral effect is a small positive trend, +0.058, 95% CI [−0.008, +0.123] — B cheats marginally more when A is desperate, but it does not reach significance.

On the aligned 27B the two weak signals are coherent — because B barely catches the emotion, the behavioral signal is faint — so this test alone cannot separate "no behavioral effect" from "no contagion to begin with." We therefore replicate on a matched pair of 8B models that differ only in alignment: an instruction-tuned Llama-3-8B and its abliterated variant (refusal training removed), the latter with a higher misbehavior baseline and no alignment to suppress an effect. Two conclusions follow. First, the honesty-floor hypothesis is ruled out: removing alignment raised the baseline (0.31 vs. 0.24) but did not uncover a behavioral effect — the emotion-specific cheat shift is near zero on the abliterated model too. Second, and more strongly, on the one setting where we can verify the emotion transmitted into B (manip +13.8, significant) and the model is willing to misbehave, B's actions still do not specifically move. The affective coupling reaches representation — verifiably — but its reach into behavior is, at most, small, and is not an artifact of alignment. We stop short of "provably zero": the contagion-arm behavioral CI is wide ([−0.08, +0.13], n = 32) and does not exclude a small effect; the induction-arm estimate is tighter and centered on zero.

11Discussion and limitations

What the results say together. Affective coupling between LLM agents is real (§5), content-mediated and not surgically removable from text (§6), a clean measurable channel under activation control (§7–8, 0.197 nats), and expressible in a fixed verbalizable basis (§9) — but it does not detectably reach behavior (§10), even in an unaligned model where contagion into the receiving agent is verified and misbehavior is unconstrained. The gap between a strong, clearly-measurable representational coupling and an undetectable behavioral one is, we think, the most interesting object here: an agent's affective state is readable and monitorable even when its behavioral consequence through a peer is not. We flag the fragility of behavioral estimates in this regime: an underpowered pilot (n = 8) produced an unstable value (−0.001 to +0.117 across phrasings), and only the powered, matched-model runs are reliable — a caution for this whole line of work.

Limitations. (i) Primary results are single-model; the 8B check reproduces the qualitative story but numbers differ. (ii) The behavioral null is for one family of pressure scenarios and one honesty baseline; a less-aligned agent might behave differently. (iii) The verbalizable readout depends on a hand-specified emotion vocabulary and a chosen mid layer. (iv) The MI estimate is Gaussian and low-dimensional by construction; it lower-bounds any nonlinear coupling. (v) The activation-passing channel is a constructed probe, not a naturally-occurring one — it demonstrates measurability, not that agents pass activations in the wild.

Reproducibility note. The experiments ran on ephemeral cloud sandboxes that terminated mid-run several times; we resolved this by checkpointing every result and all generations to a versioned dataset after each step, so the program completed across sessions without re-computing finished work. All results and the ≈13k archived generations are released.

12Conclusion

One language-model agent's emotion reaches another's internal representation — measurably (0.197 nats), verifiably content-mediated, and readable in words for every emotion tested. It does not, on present evidence, reach the other's behavior: in the one setting where contagion into the receiving agent is verified — an unaligned model, off any honesty floor — the emotion still produces no significant behavioral shift, and removing alignment does not uncover one. Affective coupling is a robust, quantifiable, interpretable representation-level phenomenon in multi-agent LLM systems, with a behavioral reach that is at most small and, as measured here, undetectable. That an agent's affective state is readable and monitorable without being a behavioral lever through a peer is, we suggest, the useful shape of the result for safety monitoring.
