# OpenReview submission metadata (IAB @ NeurIPS 2026), final 2026-09-06

**Title:** Steered affect moves a conversational partner's activations: the estimator, three pre-registered ablations, and the controls register

**TL;DR (one sentence):** Steered affect measurably moves a partner LLM's activations, but the logistic probe direction does not reproduce across disjoint halves (0.57/0.29 vs 0.97/0.94 for difference-of-means), rank-1 ablation leaves most of the response, and a permuted-label control fitted by the same estimator blocks where a random one is inert.

**Keywords:** linear probes; steering vectors; activation ablation; probe direction reproducibility; difference-of-means vs logistic regression; permuted-label control; controls for concept ablation; multi-agent LLM interaction; affect transfer between agents; pre-registered experiments; dose-response; interpretability methodology

**Abstract (plain text, from the final PDF):**

When one language-model agent is steered into an emotion and talks to another, a linear probe’s projection of the second agent’s reply activations moves with the dose, for five of six emotions on Qwen3.6-27B and on Llama-3-8B-Instruct-abliterated. We ask what carries it, and find that the standard linear tools mislead in ways their usual checks do not catch. The logisticregression direction that probe studies use to read such states reproduces across disjoint halves at only 0.57 (27B, n = 2000 per half) and 0.29 (8B), while difference-of-means reaches 0.97 and 0.94 on the same splits, and a fixed per-sample penalty does not close the gap. In the n < d regime probe studies work in, every logistic fit is a perfect separator: its direction is the penalty’s tie-break, drifting from the mean difference as n grows (cosine 0.97 to 0.63); projected onto 100 principal components, where n ≫ d′ , the fits stop separating and the direction reproduces at 0.90; the two estimators’ held-out projections still correlate at 0.90–0.96. With the reproducible direction, three pre-registered ablations test the linear account of the channel. Removing the emotion direction at every readable layer leaves a median 91% of the response against baseline (83% with the untestable emotion included) and 91% against a random-direction control (85%); a rank-5 affect subspace fails its own instrument check; a rank-5 subspace fitted by the same estimator on permuted labels blocks three emotions at BH-FDR q < 0.05 where the affect subspace blocks two (at half to two-thirds of its size on those two, and more than it on afraid), while a random 5-frame of half its footprint, a random 23-frame matched to its footprint and a permuted-label rank-1 direction are inert on every testable emotion: the control that bites is the one built from the pool’s activations, not the one that removes the most (whether its labels matter is untested). Four further label permutations of that control block angry in all four and sad in three (unadjusted intervals), but all three emotions together in only one, so the three-emotion pattern belongs to one permutation while the block on angry and sad recurs. Reestimating every direction on ten re-splits of the probe pool, sad’s block against the permuted control holds in all ten draws (median 35%, range 24–56%); afraid’s is smaller (18%, 7–23%) and its manipulation check passes in only four, so the affect-specific claim rests on sad, the one emotion that also replicates across models. The receiver’s “other-speaker” projection also rises with dose, so the probe does not separate the receiver’s state from its model of the sender’s.
